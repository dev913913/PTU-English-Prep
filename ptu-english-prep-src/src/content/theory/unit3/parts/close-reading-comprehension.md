# Close Reading and Comprehension

## What is close reading?

Close reading means reading a passage slowly and carefully, so that you understand not only its **topic** but also its **message**. The topic is what the passage is about. The message is what the writer wants you to understand about that topic.

*Example:* The topic of a passage may be "leadership". Its message may be "anyone can become a leader through effort and example".

When you read closely, you notice the main idea, the supporting details, the order of ideas, the writer's tone, and important words.

## Know your question type

Most comprehension questions belong to one of five types. Knowing the type tells you how to answer.

| Type | What it looks like | How to answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fact** | Who, what, when, where | Find the sentence and rewrite it briefly |
| **Reason** | Why, how | Give the cause or method, using *because* or *by* |
| **Inference** | What can we understand, what does the writer suggest | Use clues in the passage to work out an idea that is not stated directly |
| **Vocabulary** | What does the word or phrase mean here | Use the sentence around the word, then give a simple synonym |
| **Main idea or title** | What is the passage about, suggest a title | Give the central message in one short line |

## A reliable method: Read, Mark, Match, Write, Check

1. **Read** the whole passage once without stopping.
2. **Mark** names, dates, reasons, results, comparisons and contrast words such as **but**, **however** and **although**.
3. **Match** each question to the sentence or idea in the passage that answers it.
4. **Write** a direct answer in one or two complete sentences. Begin with words from the question.
5. **Check** that every point comes from the passage and that spelling and tense are correct.

## Practice passage 1: Leadership

> A leader is made, not born. A true leader is not necessarily the person at the helm of affairs; a true leader is one whose position others would want to emulate. Such a leader guides others either by personal example or by directing a specific course of action. A sympathetic or democratic leader understands the problems of people at the workplace and encourages teammates and colleagues. In today's competitive world, advancement depends less on the number of years served and more on actual performance and results. Outstanding work raises a person's stature in an organisation and inspires others to follow. With firm determination and effort in the right direction, every individual can aspire to become a leader.

### Questions

1. Who is a true leader?
2. Why should a leader be sympathetic and understanding?
3. How can a person become a leader?
4. What is the role of outstanding performance in an organisation?
5. How can a person aspire to become a leader?

Try these on your own first. Then open the answers.

- A true leader guides others and becomes a person whom they wish to emulate.
- Understanding other people's problems helps a leader support and encourage the team.
- Leadership can be developed through determined effort, good direction and personal example.

### A weak answer and a strong answer

**Question 2:** Why should a leader be sympathetic and understanding?

| Weak answer | Strong answer |
| --- | --- |
| "A sympathetic or democratic leader understands the problems of people at the workplace and encourages teammates and colleagues. In today's competitive world, advancement depends less on the number of years served." | "A leader should be sympathetic because understanding the problems of the people at work helps the leader to encourage and support the team." |
| Copies two sentences and includes a point that does not answer the question. | Begins with the question's words, gives the reason, and stops. |

### Vocabulary in context

You can often work out a word from the sentence around it.

| Word or phrase | Meaning in this passage | Clue in the passage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **emulate** | to follow as an example | "one whose position others would want to emulate" |
| **at the helm of affairs** | in charge, in control | A true leader is "not necessarily" this person |
| **stature** | standing or reputation | "raises a person's stature in an organisation" |
| **aspire** | to wish and work towards something | "every individual can aspire to become a leader" |

## Practice passage 2: A habit called reading

> Many students believe that reading is a task to be finished before an examination. In reality, reading is a habit that grows slowly, like physical fitness. A student who reads for twenty minutes every day soon notices that unfamiliar words become familiar, long sentences become easier to follow, and ideas begin to connect. Reading widely also improves writing, because the reader unconsciously absorbs how good writers build sentences and arrange arguments. However, the habit does not develop by accident. It needs a fixed time, a quiet place, and material that interests the reader. A student who begins with newspaper editorials, short stories or articles on a favourite subject is more likely to continue than one who forces himself through difficult books. Over time, the confidence gained through regular reading helps in every subject, not only in English.

### Questions

1. Why does the writer compare reading to physical fitness?
2. What changes does a student notice after reading daily for twenty minutes?
3. How does reading help a person's writing?
4. What three things does the reading habit need?
5. What kinds of reading material are suggested for beginners?
6. What does *unconsciously* mean in the passage?
7. Suggest a suitable title for the passage.

<details>
<summary><strong>Show the model answers</strong></summary>

1. The writer compares reading to physical fitness because both grow slowly through regular practice and do not come overnight.
2. The student notices that unfamiliar words become familiar, long sentences become easier to follow, and ideas begin to connect.
3. A reader unconsciously absorbs how good writers build sentences and arrange their arguments, and this improves the reader's own writing.
4. The habit needs a fixed time, a quiet place and material that interests the reader.
5. Beginners are advised to start with newspaper editorials, short stories or articles on a favourite subject.
6. *Unconsciously* means without deliberately trying or noticing.
7. "Reading: A Habit, Not a Task" (any title that gives the central idea is acceptable).

## Reading between the lines: inference

An **inference** is an idea that the passage suggests but does not state directly. A good inference is supported by the text. A bad inference goes beyond it.

Using passage 2:

- **Valid inference:** A student who reads regularly will probably find difficult passages less frightening over time. (The passage says reading builds confidence and makes long sentences easier.)
- **Invalid inference:** A student who does not read daily will fail the examination. (The passage never says this.)

Before you write an inference, ask: *Which line of the passage supports this?* If you cannot point to one, do not write it.

## Exam tips

- Begin the answer with words from the question, so the examiner sees at once that you have answered it.
- Use one or two sentences unless the question asks for more. Look at the marks given and write accordingly.
- Do not copy a long paragraph. Pick the lines that answer the question and put them in your own words.
- For a *title* question, choose a short phrase that covers the whole passage, not only one detail.

## Common mistakes

- Answering from general knowledge instead of from the passage
- Copying whole sentences without choosing the relevant part
- Giving a long answer that includes unrelated points
- Writing an inference that the passage does not support

> **Remember:** A comprehension answer should come from the passage. Do not add unrelated personal opinions unless the question asks for them.
